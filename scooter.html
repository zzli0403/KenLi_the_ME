Self-Balancing Remote-Controlled Scooter

Autonomous Balance & Remote Operation Prototype

ME 480 • Completed Project

Project Overview

This project explored the feasibility of enabling a two-wheel electric scooter to maintain balance and maneuver remotely without a rider onboard. Developed as part of ME 480: Introduction to Computer-Aided Technology, the prototype represents an early step toward autonomous scooter fleet repositioning and remote recovery.

The system integrates mechanical design, actuation, sensing, and embedded control to achieve dynamic stabilization and remote-controlled motion on a modified consumer scooter platform.

Motivation & Context

Shared electric scooters are widely used in urban environments, yet fleet logistics still rely heavily on manual labor to retrieve, charge, and reposition vehicles. This project investigates a technical foundation for reducing that dependency by enabling scooters to balance and move autonomously under remote supervision.

  • Reduce manual scooter retrieval and repositioning effort
  • Demonstrate feasibility of riderless balance control
  • Integrate mechanical and control systems in a real-world platform

System Architecture

The scooter uses an inertial measurement unit (IMU) to sense orientation and angular velocity, feeding balance data to an embedded controller. Actuation is achieved through servo-driven subsystems that manipulate steering, throttle, braking, and stabilization elements.

  • IMU-based state estimation for balance control
  • Embedded microcontroller handling control logic and communication
  • Servo-actuated steering, throttle, and braking mechanisms
  • Wireless remote interface for operator commands

Engineering Challenges

Balancing a two-wheel scooter without a rider introduces significant control and mechanical challenges. The absence of rider mass reduces inherent damping and increases sensitivity to disturbances, placing greater demands on actuation response and structural rigidity.

  • Maintaining stability without passive rider-induced damping
  • Managing actuator latency and mechanical backlash
  • Packaging actuators and electronics within a constrained frame

Results & Current Status

The completed prototype successfully demonstrated self-balancing behavior and remote actuation of steering and propulsion functions. While not fully autonomous, the system validates key concepts required for future development toward autonomous fleet operations.

  • Achieved stable balance under remote control
  • Demonstrated coordinated steering and throttle actuation
  • Identified clear pathways for future control and hardware refinement

Key Lessons Learned

  • Mechanical stiffness and alignment are critical to effective control performance
  • System-level integration often dominates project complexity over individual subsystems
  • Early consideration of serviceability and packaging simplifies iteration and debugging
